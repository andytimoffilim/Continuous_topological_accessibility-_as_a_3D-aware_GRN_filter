\begin{abstract}
We formalize \emph{Relative Forest Accessibility} (RFA), a topological accessibility index computed as the solution of $(I - \gamma P)x = v_0$, where $P$ is a Markov transition matrix built from Hi-C contacts and $v_0$ is a vector of TF peaks (or ATAC peaks). RFA yields a continuous accessibility profile across all genomic bins.

We test RFA on three targets. First, on promoter TF counts (GM12878 chr1, 10\,kb, 811 genes), RFA loses to 1D distance (AUC 0.836 vs 0.976); this is an artifact because the target and the 1D distance look at the same window. Second, on eQTL (GTEx v10 LCL, chr1+17+19, 10\,kb, 1625 genes), RFA \emph{anticorrelates} with eQTL: $\rho(\mathrm{RFA}, -\log_{10} p_{\min}) = -0.09$, $\mathrm{AUC}(\mathrm{RFA}) = 0.386$ (below chance), $\mathrm{AUC}(-\mathrm{RFA}) = 0.614$. High RFA corresponds to fewer eQTL, and dose-response analysis confirms that $\mathrm{AUC}(\mathrm{RFA})$ decreases with eQTL strength (mean trend $-0.056$, 19/20 TFs). A distance-preserving shuffle of the Hi-C matrix reproduces this anticorrelation ($\Delta\mathrm{AUC}$ vs real: $[-0.074, +0.076]$ across 5 TFs, all CIs include 0), whereas a degree-preserving rewiring destroys it ($\Delta\mathrm{AUC} \in [-0.261, -0.003]$, CIs exclude 0 for 5/5 TFs); the anticorrelation therefore reflects \emph{distance-decay of Hi-C contacts (local 3D accessibility)}, not higher-order topology. The anticorrelation replicates on chr17 ($\Delta\rho=-0.13$ for rewired, 0/5 for shuffled) and on chr19 with an inverted control pattern, consistent with local within-diagonal heterogeneity as the carrier on short gene-dense chromosomes. Third, on $\Delta$RNA (MCF10A vs MCF7, 40\,kb, ATAC peaks), RFA does not predict $\Delta$RNA ($\rho = +0.005$, permutation $p=0.87$, bootstrap 95\% CI $[-0.040, +0.053]$, $n=1730$); the CI includes zero and does not exclude the practical boundary $|\rho|=0.05$. Within-line sanity checks are also null.

We conclude that RFA anticorrelates with eQTL via local distance-decay and does not predict $\Delta$RNA. The anticorrelation is carried by distance-decay, not by loops or TADs, and may reflect expression buffering: genes with strong eQTL are less dependent on local 3D accessibility. RFA is not a universal predictor but a filter with an inverted sign on eQTL and no signal on $\Delta$RNA.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Problem}
Existing 3D-aware TF$\rightarrow$gene filters (SSD 2017, Esearch3D 2023, ChIPSP 2026) use either a binary (loop yes/no) or a scalar (direct contact, 1D distance). RFA offers a continuous accessibility profile. The key question is: \emph{where does RFA give signal, in which direction, and what is the mechanism}.

\subsection{Positioning}
We position RFA not as a ``new framework'' but as ``continuous topological accessibility as a resolution-dependent 3D-aware GRN filter''. RFA is not a universal predictor but a filter with a clearly delineated applicability domain and a \emph{signal direction}.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Formalization of RFA via a Markov random walk (Section~\ref{sec:theory}).
    \item \textbf{Anticorrelation of RFA with eQTL} ($\mathrm{AUC}(-\mathrm{RFA})=0.614$, dose-response trend $-0.056$).
    \item \textbf{Negative result on $\Delta$RNA} ($\rho=0.005$, MDR$=0.067$).
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} the eQTL anticorrelation is carried by distance-decay (topology controls, Section~\ref{sec:topology_controls}), not by higher-order topology.
    \item \textbf{Methodological warning:} an automatic sign flip in AUC can mask anticorrelation as ``high AUC''; dose-response reveals it.
.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Theory}\label{sec:theory}

We formalize the computation of a \emph{topological accessibility index} (RFA) that quantifies how accessible a genomic locus is to a transcription factor (TF) given the 3D chromatin architecture measured by Hi-C. The construction proceeds in four stages: from Hi-C contact counts to a weighted graph; from the graph to a Markov transition matrix; from the transition matrix to a continuous accessibility profile; from the profile to per-gene scores and validation statistics. Throughout, the Markov chain is a \emph{mathematical instrument for deconvolving spatial noise and accounting for multi-step paths}, not a kinetic model of TF motion.

\subsection{Hi-C Graph Construction}

Let $C \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n \times n}$ be a symmetric Hi-C contact matrix at resolution $r$ (e.g., $r = 10\,\mathrm{kb}$), where $n$ is the number of genomic bins  measured by Hi-C \cite{dekker2013,rao2014}. We apply:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Diagonal zeroing}: $C_{ii} := 0$ for all $i$, eliminating self-contacts that would otherwise dominate the degree distribution.
    \item \textbf{Top-$k$ sparsification}: For each row $i$, retain only the $k$ largest entries, yielding a sparse matrix $\tilde{A}$. This replaces dense noise with the strongest topological signals. Statistical treatment of Hi-C noise and sparsification is discussed
in \cite{hu2013}.
    \item \textbf{Symmetrization}: $A := \max(\tilde{A}, \tilde{A}^\top)$, preserving symmetry.
\end{enumerate}

A/B compartments (the dominant large-scale chromatin structure) are captured by the first principal component of $\log(1+C)$. To isolate fine-scale topological structure from compartment signal, we regress each row of $A$ on the compartment score and retain residuals:
\begin{equation}
A_{ij}^{\mathrm{res}} = A_{ij} - \big(\beta_0^{(i)} + \beta_1^{(i)} \mathrm{PC1}_j\big), \qquad A_{ij}^{\mathrm{res}} := \max(A_{ij}^{\mathrm{res}}, 0),
\end{equation}
where $(\beta_0^{(i)}, \beta_1^{(i)})$ are obtained by ordinary least squares on the support of row $i$.

\subsection{Markov Transition Matrix}

Define the degree vector $d_i = \sum_j A_{ij}$ and the diagonal matrix $D = \mathrm{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_n)$. The row-stochastic transition matrix is
\begin{equation}
P = D^{-1} A, \qquad P_{ij} = \frac{A_{ij}}{d_i}, \qquad \sum_j P_{ij} = 1.
\end{equation}
The matrix $P$ encodes the probability that a random walker at bin $i$ moves to bin $j$ in one step.

\begin{remark}
The Markov chain is \emph{not} a kinetic model of TF motion. It is a graph-theoretic object whose powers $P^t$ aggregate the contributions of all paths of length $t$ through the chromatin graph.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}
The matrix $P$ has the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item After adding self-loops to zero-degree bins (bins with no Hi-C contacts after top-$k$ sparsification, PC1 residualization, and non-negative clipping), $P$ is row-stochastic: $P \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}$. Without this correction, $P$ has zero rows (8.6\% of bins on chr1, 3.7\% on chr17, 5.1\% on chr19) and is only sub-stochastic; we apply the correction throughout.
    \item The stationary distribution $\pi$ satisfies $\pi P = \pi$, with $\pi_i = d_i / \sum_j d_j$ (degree-weighted).
    \item For the \emph{raw} symmetrized matrix $A$, $P$ is similar to a symmetric matrix: $D^{1/2} P D^{-1/2} = D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2}$, so all eigenvalues are real and lie in $[-1,1]$. After PC1 residualization and non-negative clipping, $A^{\mathrm{res}}$ is not symmetric ($\max|A^{\mathrm{res}} - (A^{\mathrm{res}})^\top| = 12.5$ on chr1), so eigenvalues of $P$ may be complex. The Neumann series still converges because $\rho(P) \le 1$ for any non-negative matrix with row sums $\le 1$; the real-eigenvalue guarantee is lost.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Relative Forest Accessibility (RFA)}

Given a source distribution $v_0 \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^n$ (e.g., TF binding peaks in bins), we define the RFA profile as
\begin{equation}
x_\gamma := (I - \gamma P)^{-1} v_0 = \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t P^t v_0,
\label{eq:rfa}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma \in (0,1)$ is a damping factor.

\begin{proposition}[Convergence]
The Neumann series \eqref{eq:rfa} converges for all $\gamma \in (0,1)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $\|P\|_\infty = 1$ and $\rho(P) \leq 1$, we have $\|(\gamma P)^t\|_\infty \leq \gamma^t$, and $\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t = 1/(1-\gamma) < \infty$.
\end{proof}

The profile $x_\gamma$ has three equivalent interpretations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Path sum}: $x_{\gamma,i} = \sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t (P^t v_0)_i$ is a weighted sum over all paths of length $t$ from peaks to bin $i$, with exponential decay in path length.
    \item \textbf{Steady-state with restart}: $x_\gamma$ is the unique solution of $(I - \gamma P) x = v_0$, equivalent to a random walk with restart (RWR) at rate $1-\gamma$.
    \item \textbf{Forest accessibility}: $x_{\gamma,i}$ is proportional to the total weight of spanning forests of the graph rooted at the peak set, with edge weights weighted by $\gamma$.
\end{enumerate}

The parameter $\gamma$ controls the \emph{effective path length}. For $\gamma \to 1$, $(1-\gamma)x_\gamma$ approaches the constant vector $(\pi\cdot v_0)/(\pi\cdot\mathbf{1})\cdot\mathbf{1}$, where $\pi$ is the stationary distribution of $P$; the unnormalized $x_\gamma$ itself diverges, and the normalized limit is a scalar multiple of $\mathbf{1}$, not $\pi$. We tested $\gamma \in \{0.5, 0.7, 0.85, 0.95\}$; ... At 40\,kb with ATAC peaks, RFA gives no signal (bootstrap 95\% CI for $\rho$ includes zero and does not exclude $|\rho|=0.05$), consistent with the hypothesis that the signal lives at fine scales and is sensitive to the type of $v_0$.

\subsection{Per-Gene Scoring and Baselines}

Let $\mathcal{G}$ be the set of genes with a promoter annotation. For each gene $g$, let $b(g)$ be the bin containing its promoter ($b(g) = \lfloor \mathrm{start}(g)/r \rfloor$). Define
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RFA}(g) := x_{\gamma, b(g)}.
\end{equation}

We compare RFA against three baselines:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{1D distance}: $D_{1D}(g) = \min_{p \in \mathrm{peaks}} |\mathrm{start}(g) - p|$.
    \item \textbf{Direct contact (ChIPSP proxy)}: $\mathrm{DC}(g) = \max_{p \in \mathrm{peaks}} A_{b(g), p}$.
    \item \textbf{Combined model}: $\mathrm{Comb}(g) = z(\mathrm{RFA}(g)) + z(\mathrm{DC}(g))$, where $z(\cdot)$ is z-score normalization.
\end{enumerate}

For a continuous target $y_g$, we report Spearman $\rho(\mathrm{RFA}, y)$ and partial $\rho(\mathrm{RFA}, y \mid D_{1D}, \mathrm{DC})$, obtained by rank-residualizing both variables against the controls. For binary classification $y_g \in \{0,1\}$, we report ROC-AUC, with DeLong-style bootstrap ($B=1000$) for $\Delta \mathrm{AUC}$.

\subsection{Statistical Properties}

\textbf{Why does RFA outperform direct contact on promoter count?} At high resolution (10\,kb), direct contacts are noisy: each bin contains few counts, and $\mathrm{DC}(g)$ has high variance. The RFA profile is a smoothed version of $v_0$ obtained by convolving along the graph structure; its variance is reduced by averaging over all paths. Let $v_0$ be a noisy indicator of peaks and $\mu_0 = \mathbb{E}[v_0]$. Then
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[x_\gamma] = (I-\gamma P)^{-1} \mu_0, \qquad \mathrm{Var}[x_\gamma] = (I-\gamma P)^{-1} \mathrm{Var}[v_0] \big((I-\gamma P)^{-1}\big)^\top.
\end{equation}
The operator $(I-\gamma P)^{-1}$ has eigenvalues $1/(1-\gamma\lambda_k)$, where $\lambda_k$ are eigenvalues of $P$ in $[-1,1]$. Along the stationary mode ($\lambda_1=1$) the gain is $1/(1-\gamma)$, which diverges as $\gamma\to 1$; along fast modes ($|\lambda_k|\ll 1$) the gain is close to 1. Empirically, $\mathrm{Var}[x_\gamma]/\mathrm{Var}[v_0]$ \emph{grows} with $\gamma$ (1.7 at $\gamma=0.5$, 8.4 at $\gamma=0.85$, 684 at $\gamma=0.99$; Appendix~S3). RFA is therefore \emph{not} a variance-reducing smoother; it amplifies the stationary-mode component of $v_0$ and preserves fast-mode components, i.e., it re-weights paths by their length. The smoothing intuition should be read as ``re-weighting by path length'', not as ``variance reduction''.

\textbf{Why does RFA anticorrelate with eQTL?} The smoothing argument above explains why RFA outperforms DC on promoter count, but it does \emph{not} predict anticorrelation with eQTL. Empirically, $\mathrm{AUC}(\mathrm{RFA}) = 0.386$ and $\mathrm{AUC}(-\mathrm{RFA}) = 0.614$ on eQTL, and $\mathrm{AUC}(\mathrm{RFA})$ decreases with eQTL strength (Section~\ref{sec:dose_response}). Topology controls (Section~\ref{sec:topology_controls}) narrow the mechanism: a distance-preserving shuffle reproduces the anticorrelation, whereas a degree-preserving rewiring destroys it. The anticorrelation therefore reflects distance-decay of Hi-C contacts (local 3D accessibility), not loops or TADs. We interpret this as follows: eQTL genes are expressed, but with stronger eQTL they are more \emph{buffered}, i.e., less dependent on local 3D accessibility. RFA, being a distance-decay-weighted accessibility score, is higher for genes whose expression is more strongly driven by local 3D contacts, and lower for buffered genes with strong eQTL. This is a hypothesis, supported by the topology controls, and it is stated as such in Section~6.3.

\textbf{Why does the combined model fail?} Let $X = \mathrm{RFA}$ and $Y = \mathrm{DC}$. Suppose $X = S(Y) + \varepsilon$ where $S$ is smoothing and $\varepsilon$ is noise. Then $\mathrm{AUC}(X+Y) \leq \mathrm{AUC}(X)$ whenever $\varepsilon$ dominates, because $Y$ reintroduces the noise $\varepsilon$ that $S$ removed. On eQTL, the combined model also fails ($\mathrm{AUC}=0.561$ vs $\mathrm{RFA}$ alone), but for a different reason: RFA and DC anticorrelate with eQTL in different ways, so $z(\mathrm{RFA}) + z(\mathrm{DC})$ partially cancels the anticorrelation.

\section{Data}

\subsection{Hi-C}
We use GM12878 in situ Hi-C (ENCODE/4DN, \texttt{4DNFIZGCA8AH.hic}) at 10\,kb resolution \cite{rao2014} for chromosomes 1, 17, and 19, extracted with \texttt{juicer\_tools dump observed NONE} and converted to dense \texttt{.npy} (int32) via a sparse-triplet converter. For MCF10A and MCF7, we use 40\,kb matrices for the same chromosomes (\texttt{MCF\{10A,7\}\_chr\{1,17,19\}\_40kb\_fixed.npy}). The 10\,kb matrices have 24\,895, 8\,325, and 5\,861 bins for chr1, chr17, and chr19 respectively, with nnz/$n^2$ of 0.012, 0.030, and 0.044; the sizes match chromosome lengths.

\subsection{TFBS (GM12878)}
Peaks are taken from \texttt{tfbs\_GM12878.bed} (BED6). We test 20 TFs: IKZF1, CTCF, SMC3, RAD21, PAX5, EBF1, EP300, POLR2A, YY1, GABPA, MAX, E2F4, ELF1, TCF12, BATF, BCL11A, IRF4, MEF2A, NRF1, and ZNF143.

\subsection{ATAC peaks (MCF10A / MCF7)}
For MCF7 we use IDR-thresholded peaks from GEO GSE169929 (\texttt{GSE169929\_ENCFF882OVP\_...bed.gz}); for MCF10A, broadPeak calls from GEO GSM3735772. Only the first three columns (chrom, start, end) are used; $v_0$ is the indicator of bins containing an ATAC peak. ATAC peaks mark accessible regulatory DNA \cite{thurman2012}.

\subsection{eQTL (GTEx v10 LCL)}
We use \texttt{QTD000221.all.tsv.gz} from the eQTL Catalogue (EBI), corresponding to GTEx v10 LCL (study \texttt{QTS000015}, dataset \texttt{QTD000221}), \texttt{quant\_method=ge}, \texttt{sample\_size=326}. The file is streamed in 2M-row chunks; for each gene we record $p_{\min}$ and $n_{\mathrm{sig}}$ (number of variants with $p<5\times10^{-8}$), and define $\mathrm{eqtl\_bin} = \mathbb{1}[n_{\mathrm{sig}}>0]$. The result is 18\,988 genes, of which 1\,567 (8.3\%) have at least one significant variant. The eQTL Catalogue provides uniformly reprocessed summary statistics
\cite{kerimov2021}.

\subsection{RNA (MCF10A / MCF7)}
We use \texttt{MCF10A\_RNA.tsv} (16\,829 genes) and \texttt{MCF7\_RNA.tsv} (19\,154 genes), with columns \texttt{gene\_id}, \texttt{gene\_name}, and \texttt{TPM}; there are no NAs and gene IDs are without version suffixes.

\subsection{Gene annotation}
Promoters are taken from \texttt{gene\_structure\_hg38.csv} (\texttt{element\_type=='promoter'}, first promoter per gene, \texttt{chrom} without \texttt{chr}). This yields 1043, 538, and 739 promoters on chr1, chr17, and chr19.

\subsection{Promoter TF count}
The promoter count matrix \texttt{matrix\_with\_hic\_enhancers.csv} contains 811 genes on chr1 with TF peak counts in the window $[-2000, +200]$\,bp from TSS (not score sums). It was built for chr1 only; for chr17/19 we merge directly with the eQTL target.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Pipeline}
The pipeline is:
\begin{verbatim}
Hi-C .npy -> build_markov_matrix(topk=300, PC1-residualize) -> P
v0 (TF peaks / ATAC peaks) -> rfa_profile(P, v0, gamma=0.85) -> x_gamma
x_gamma[bin(gene)] -> RFA(gene)
\end{verbatim}

The same flow is shown schematically in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}.

\subsection{Metrics}
We use ROC-AUC for binary targets; Spearman $\rho$ for continuous targets; partial Spearman by rank-residualizing both variables on controls; bootstrap CI (1000 resamples, gene-level resampling) for $\Delta$AUC, partial, \textbf{and Spearman $\rho$}; permutation $p$ (500 permutations) for $\rho$ and AUC; and MDR (minimum detectable $\rho$) via Fisher $z$: $\tanh((z_\alpha + z_\beta)/\sqrt{n-3})$. A practical significance boundary $|\rho|=0.05$ is pre-specified. A CI is said to \emph{exclude} the boundary only if both CI limits lie strictly inside $(-0.05, +0.05)$; otherwise the boundary is \emph{not excluded}.

\textbf{Direction check.} Both $\mathrm{AUC}(\mathrm{RFA})$ and $\mathrm{AUC}(-\mathrm{RFA})$ are reported. If $\mathrm{AUC}(\mathrm{RFA})<0.5<\mathrm{AUC}(-\mathrm{RFA})$, RFA anticorrelates with the target. \textbf{No sign flip is applied} --- a flip would mask anticorrelation. In an earlier version of the pipeline, an automatic flip produced ``AUC=0.611''; in the final dose-response analysis the flip is removed.

\subsection{Controls}
We use 1D distance, direct contact (dc), PC1 (compartment), baseline RNA, permutation null, within-line sanity, the direction check, and topology controls (degree-preserving rewiring, distance-preserving shuffle, flattened graph; Section~\ref{sec:topology_controls}).

\subsection{Topology controls: distance-decay, not loops}\label{sec:topology_controls}
To determine whether the eQTL anticorrelation is driven by higher-order topology or by local distance-decay, we compared real Hi-C against three controls on chr1, 10\,kb, for 5 TFs (IKZF1, CTCF, EP300, POLR2A, YY1). All graphs were passed through the same pipeline (PC1 residualization, non-negative clipping, self-loop on zero-degree bins, $P=D^{-1}A$, $\gamma=0.85$).

\emph{Degree-preserving rewiring} (\texttt{double\_edge\_swap}, $10\times$\texttt{nnz} swaps) keeps the degree sequence but randomizes edges across all distances. It destroys the anticorrelation: $\mathrm{AUC}(\mathrm{RFA})$ moves toward 0.5 (from 0.30--0.36 to 0.39--0.51), $\Delta\mathrm{AUC} \in [-0.261, -0.003]$ with CIs excluding 0 for 5/5 TFs.

\emph{Distance-preserving shuffle} keeps the distribution of contacts on each diagonal $|i-j|=d$ but randomizes positions within diagonals. It \emph{reproduces} the anticorrelation: $\mathrm{AUC}(\mathrm{RFA}) = 0.305$--$0.371$ vs real $0.300$--$0.361$, $\Delta\mathrm{AUC} \in [-0.074, +0.076]$ with all CIs including 0.

\emph{Flattened graph} (configuration model with the same degree sequence) is intermediate: $\mathrm{AUC}(\mathrm{RFA}) = 0.354$--$0.438$, $\Delta\mathrm{AUC} \in [-0.202, +0.033]$, significant only for EP300.

We conclude that RFA's eQTL signal is explained by distance-decay of Hi-C contacts (local 3D accessibility), not by long-range topological structure. This is consistent with the interpretation that RFA measures a \emph{local} accessibility profile rather than a global topological property.

\subsection{Replication on chr17 and chr19}\label{sec:topology_chr17_19}

The anticorrelation replicates on both additional chromosomes: real RFA gives
$\mathrm{AUC}(\mathrm{RFA})=0.368$ (chr17) and $0.401$ (chr19),
$\rho(\mathrm{RFA}, -\log_{10}p_{\min}) = -0.106$ and $-0.120$,
with $\mathrm{AUC}(\mathrm{RFA})<0.5$ for 5/5 TFs on each.
Because the number of eQTL-positive genes is small (chr17: 398 genes;
chr19: 547 genes; $\approx 30$--$50$ positives per chromosome), AUC-based
$\Delta$ statistics are underpowered
($\mathrm{MDR}(\Delta\mathrm{AUC})\approx 0.10$--$0.15$ versus observed
$\Delta\mathrm{AUC}\approx 0.05$--$0.07$), so we report
$\rho$ as the primary metric on these chromosomes.

\textbf{chr17 replicates the chr1 mechanism.}
Degree-preserving rewiring ($10\times$\texttt{nnz} swaps) removes the
anticorrelation by $\rho$: $\Delta\rho = -0.125$ (IKZF1),
$-0.125$ (POLR2A), $-0.130$ (YY1), with CIs excluding zero for 3/5 TFs;
mean $\rho$ moves from $-0.106$ (real) to $-0.004$ (rewired), i.e.\ a shift
of $+0.101$ toward zero. Distance-preserving shuffle preserves it:
0/5 TFs significant, mean $\rho=-0.061$ (shift $+0.045$).
The rewired shift is $2.3\times$ larger than the shuffled shift,
matching the chr1 pattern.

\textbf{chr19 shows an inverted pattern, consistent with local
within-diagonal heterogeneity.}
Here the roles reverse: distance-preserving shuffle removes the
anticorrelation (4/5 TFs significant; mean $\rho$ moves from $-0.120$ to
$-0.058$, shift $+0.063$), while degree-preserving rewiring preserves it
(0/5 TFs significant; mean $\rho=-0.099$, shift $+0.021$).
This is not a power artefact --- the shuffle effect is significant ---
but a chromosome-specific effect. chr19 is short (5861 bins versus 24895
on chr1) and gene-dense; on such a chromosome the distance-preserving
shuffle destroys the \emph{local within-diagonal heterogeneity} that
carries the anticorrelation, whereas degree-preserving rewiring, which
redistributes edges across all distances while retaining sparsity,
partially preserves it. The chr19 result therefore does not contradict
distance-decay as the mechanism; it refines it: on short gene-dense
chromosomes the relevant structure is finer than the diagonal distance
itself.

\textbf{Summary.}
The eQTL anticorrelation replicates on chr17 and chr19 (real
$\mathrm{AUC}<0.5$ and $\rho<0$ for 5/5 TFs on each). The
distance-decay mechanism is positively replicated on chr17
(rewired kills, shuffled preserves) and inverted on chr19, where local
within-diagonal heterogeneity, rather than distance itself, is the carrier.
The chr1 full-power analysis (Section~\ref{sec:topology_controls}) remains
the primary evidence; chr17/19 provide the replication and the refinement.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What RFA does}
RFA anticorrelates with eQTL at 10\,kb with TF peaks ($\mathrm{AUC}(-\mathrm{RFA})=0.614$, dose-response trend $-0.056$, 19/20 TFs). It is not a proxy for direct contact ($\rho(\mathrm{RFA},\mathrm{dc})=0.58$, 0/20 $>0.8$) and not a proxy for compartments (partial RFA $\mid$ PC1 $=+0.35$). Topology controls (Section~\ref{sec:topology_controls}) show that the anticorrelation is carried by distance-decay of Hi-C contacts, not by higher-order topology.

\subsection{What RFA does not do}
RFA does not predict $\Delta$RNA at 40\,kb with ATAC peaks ($\rho=0.005$, MDR$=0.067$). It does not add over 1D on promoter count (partial $\mid$ 1D $= -0.068$). It does not predict eQTL in the positive direction --- only anticorrelates.

\subsection{Why anticorrelation?}
The topology controls (Section~\ref{sec:topology_controls}) narrow the mechanism: the anticorrelation is carried by the distance-decay of Hi-C contacts, not by higher-order topology. This is consistent with (i) \emph{expression buffering}: genes with strong eQTL are less dependent on local 3D accessibility, and RFA --- a distance-decay-weighted accessibility score --- is lower for them. It is also consistent with (ii) \emph{RFA captures expression status indirectly}: eQTL genes are expressed, and the distance-decay component of RFA tracks local chromatin openness, which correlates with expression. Hypothesis (iii) \emph{target artifact} is weakened by the shuffle control: if the anticorrelation were a pure layer-mismatch artifact, the shuffle would not reproduce it. We retain (i) and (ii) as the leading interpretations and state (iii) as a residual possibility.

\subsection{Limitations}
First, resolution: 10\,kb is the working range; 40\,kb is not. Second, $v_0$: TF peaks give signal (anticorrelation); ATAC peaks do not. Third, target: eQTL is a genetic layer; $\Delta$RNA is dynamic and noisy.Fourth, PC1 residualization is applied at two levels: (a) row-wise on the Hi-C matrix $A$ inside \texttt{build\_markov\_matrix} (common to all analyses), and (b) post-hoc on the RFA profile $x_\gamma$ in \texttt{chips\_baseline\_full.py}, where $\|x^{\mathrm{res}}_\gamma\|_2/\|x_\gamma\|_2 = 0.623$ on average on the promoter-count benchmark (GM12878 chr1, 10\,kb, 20 TFs, 811 genes). The number 0.623 is specific to that benchmark; the eQTL pipeline (\texttt{eqtl\_multi\_chr.py}, \texttt{eqtl\_dose\_response.py}) does not apply post-hoc residualization to $x_\gamma$, and the relevant PC1 diagnostic for eQTL is the partial RFA $\mid$ PC1 value in Table~2 ($-0.03$). Fifth, single cell line/tissue: GM12878 + LCL; MCF10A/7. Sixth, sign flip: the automatic flip in the earlier pipeline masked anticorrelation; the final analysis uses explicit direction. Seventh, topology controls cover three chromosomes with 5 TFs each:
chr1 (full-power, primary evidence; Section~\ref{sec:topology_controls}),
chr17 (replicates the distance-decay mechanism by $\rho$;
Section~\ref{sec:topology_chr17_19}), and chr19 (inverted pattern;
consistent with local within-diagonal heterogeneity as the carrier on
short gene-dense chromosomes). AUC-based $\Delta$ statistics are
underpowered on chr17/19 ($n_{\mathrm{pos}}\approx 30$--$50$), so $\rho$
is the primary metric there. A full 20-TF replication is left for future
work.

\subsection{Positioning}
``Continuous \emph{local} 3D accessibility as a resolution-dependent GRN filter'': RFA anticorrelates with eQTL at 10\,kb with TF peaks, and this anticorrelation is carried by the distance-decay of Hi-C contacts rather than by higher-order topology (Section~\ref{sec:topology_controls}). RFA does not predict $\Delta$RNA at 40\,kb with ATAC. The applicability domain is static eQTL association at 10\,kb with TF peaks, and the mechanism is local 3D accessibility, not loops or TADs.

\section{Conclusion}
RFA anticorrelates with eQTL at 10\,kb with TF peaks ($\mathrm{AUC}(\mathrm{RFA})=0.386$, $\mathrm{AUC}(-\mathrm{RFA})=0.614$, dose-response trend $-0.056$), and topology controls show that this anticorrelation is carried by distance-decay of Hi-C contacts (local 3D accessibility), not by higher-order topology. RFA does not predict $\Delta$RNA at 40\,kb with ATAC peaks ($\rho=0.005$, permutation $p=0.87$, bootstrap 95\% CI $[-0.040, +0.053]$); the CI includes zero and does not exclude $|\rho|=0.05$. The anticorrelation may reflect expression buffering. The negative $\Delta$RNA result delineates the applicability boundary: RFA measures static local accessibility, not dynamics. A methodological warning follows: a sign flip in AUC masks anticorrelation, so dose-response and explicit direction are mandatory.
